Drug Interactions: Why Can One Medicine Affect Another?

Understand medicine–medicine, food, alcohol, supplement and health-condition interactions, their pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic mechanisms, and how to reduce risk.

Englishবাংলা

By Sayedul Abrar, pharmacist · 7 December 2026

In simple terms: A drug interaction happens when another medicine, food, drink, supplement or health condition changes a medicine's effect or safety. An interaction can make treatment stronger, weaker or simply different—and not every interaction is harmful.

1. What is a drug interaction?

A drug interaction occurs when a medicine is affected by another substance or by a health condition. The result may be a change in how much medicine reaches the bloodstream, how long it remains in the body, or how strongly it acts. This can increase side effects, reduce benefit or sometimes produce a useful effect when the combination is deliberately selected and monitored.

Interactions are not limited to prescription medicines. They can involve over-the-counter (OTC) products, vitamins, minerals, herbal remedies, supplements, foods, beverages, tobacco smoke and medical conditions. The exact effect depends on the products, doses, timing and the person taking them.

2. The two main mechanisms

Pharmacokinetic interactions: what the body does to a medicine

A pharmacokinetic interaction changes the absorption, distribution, metabolism or excretion (ADME) of a medicine. It may raise or lower the amount of medicine available at its site of action.

  • Absorption: One medicine may bind to another in the gut or change stomach acidity, reducing how much is absorbed. For example, some antacids can reduce the absorption of certain antibiotics; the required separation interval is product-specific.
  • Distribution: A medicine may alter how another is transported or distributed in the body. Changes in protein binding can occur, although a change in protein binding alone does not always produce a clinically important effect.
  • Metabolism: Some medicines inhibit or induce enzymes such as cytochrome P450 (CYP), changing the breakdown of another medicine. Inhibition can increase exposure to a susceptible medicine; induction can lower exposure and reduce its effect. Grapefruit affects intestinal CYP3A4 for selected medicines, not every medicine.
  • Excretion: One medicine can affect the kidney or liver handling of another, changing how quickly it leaves the body.

These pathways connect with the ADME process and drug metabolism. An enzyme or transporter interaction matters clinically only when the change is large enough, lasts long enough and affects a medicine for which that change is important.

Pharmacodynamic interactions: what medicines do to the body

A pharmacodynamic interaction occurs when medicines have additive, opposing or otherwise interacting effects at their sites of action. For example, combining two medicines that cause drowsiness may increase impairment. A combination that affects blood clotting through different pathways may increase bleeding risk. In other cases, one medicine may oppose the effect of another. These effects can occur even when the blood concentration of each medicine does not change.

3. Common types of interactions

Medicine–medicine interactions

Two or more medicines can affect each other's concentration or effects. A sedative taken with some antihistamines may cause greater drowsiness and slower reactions. Some combinations are intentionally prescribed, but may require dose selection, counselling or monitoring. Do not assume that medicines are safe to combine simply because each was prescribed separately.

Medicine–food and medicine–drink interactions

Food can change the rate or amount of absorption, and some products have specific instructions about taking them with or without meals. Grapefruit or grapefruit juice can affect selected medicines by inhibiting intestinal CYP3A4. Alcohol can increase drowsiness or slow reactions with some medicines. These warnings are not universal: check the instructions for the exact medicine rather than avoiding entire food groups without a reason.

Medicine–supplement and herbal interactions

Vitamins, minerals, botanicals and other supplements can alter absorption, metabolism or excretion. St John's wort, for example, can reduce the effectiveness of several medicines, including some hormonal contraceptives and transplant medicines. Ginkgo, aspirin and warfarin may contribute to bleeding risk when used together. “Natural” does not automatically mean safe, and supplement strength or ingredients may vary.

Medicine–condition interactions

An existing health condition can make a medicine less suitable or increase the chance of harm. For example, some nasal decongestants may be a concern for people with high blood pressure. Kidney or liver impairment can also change how certain medicines are processed or eliminated. Tell the prescriber or pharmacist about relevant diagnoses and changes in your health.

4. Are all drug interactions dangerous?

No. Some interactions are minor, some are clinically important, some can be managed with monitoring or a treatment adjustment, and some combinations should be avoided. Certain combinations are used deliberately because the combined effect is beneficial. The word interaction alone does not tell you how serious it is.

Clinical significance depends on the medicine involved, the size and duration of the change, dose, timing, the person's age and organ function, and the medicine's safety margin. A potential interaction listed in a database is a reason to review the combination—not proof that harm will occur in every person.

5. How can you reduce the risk?

  • Keep one up-to-date list of all medicines you use, including prescription and OTC products, vitamins, minerals, herbal products and supplements.
  • Show the list to your doctor, pharmacist or other healthcare professional when a medicine is started, stopped or changed. Mention relevant foods, drinks, smoking and health conditions.
  • Read the label and patient information leaflet each time you use a medicine. Check active ingredients, warnings and instructions about food, alcohol and other medicines.
  • Ask before adding a new medicine, pain reliever, cold remedy, supplement or herbal product. Different brand names can contain the same active ingredient.
  • Follow product-specific timing instructions. Do not create your own spacing schedule because interaction management varies by medicine.
  • Do not stop, skip, double or change a prescribed dose on your own after seeing an interaction alert. Ask a pharmacist or prescriber to assess the exact combination.

If you develop a severe or rapidly worsening reaction after taking medicines together—such as difficulty breathing, collapse or swelling of the face or throat—seek emergency medical care. For other new or unexpected symptoms, contact a healthcare professional promptly and provide the names and amounts of the products taken.

6. A practical example: why a complete medicine list matters

Imagine someone who takes a prescribed medicine, buys a cold remedy from a pharmacy and also uses a herbal supplement. If the prescriber only knows about the prescription medicine, the review may miss a relevant interaction. Sharing the full list—including products used only occasionally—gives the healthcare professional a more accurate picture. The goal is not to avoid every combination; it is to identify which combinations need no action, which need monitoring and which should be changed.

Remember

Drug interactions can change a medicine's effect or safety through pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic mechanisms. They may involve medicines, food, alcohol, supplements or health conditions. A complete and updated medicine list, careful label reading and a pharmacist or prescriber review are practical ways to reduce avoidable risk.

Frequently asked questions

What are the main types of drug interactions?

Common categories include medicine–medicine, medicine–food or drink, medicine–supplement or herbal product, and medicine–condition interactions. Mechanistically, many are pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic.

What is the difference between pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic interactions?

Pharmacokinetic interactions change a medicine's absorption, distribution, metabolism or excretion. Pharmacodynamic interactions change or combine the effects of medicines at their sites of action.

Are all drug interactions harmful?

No. Some are minor, some need monitoring or a change in treatment, and some are deliberately used for a beneficial effect. The significance depends on the exact medicines and the person taking them.

Can food or herbal supplements interact with medicines?

Yes. Certain foods, drinks and supplements can change medicine absorption, metabolism or effects. Grapefruit and St John's wort are examples that affect selected medicines; the interaction is not the same for every product.

Should I stop a medicine if I see an interaction warning?

No. Do not stop or change a prescribed medicine on your own. Ask your pharmacist or prescriber to review the exact medicines, doses and circumstances.

What information should I share with a pharmacist?

Share a current list of prescription medicines, OTC products, vitamins, minerals, herbal products and supplements, along with relevant health conditions and any food or alcohol instructions you have been given.
