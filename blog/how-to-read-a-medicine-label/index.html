How to Read a Medicine Label: Strength, Dose, Expiry and Storage

A practical guide to reading a medicine pack, including generic name, strength, directions, warnings and expiry.

Englishবাংলা

By Sayedul Abrar, pharmacist · 15 November 2026

Quick guide: Before using a medicine, confirm its name and active ingredient, strength, dosage form, route, directions, warnings, expiry date, batch number and storage instructions. If anything is unclear, ask a pharmacist—never guess a dose.

1. Know which label you are reading

A medicine may come with several information sources: the outer carton, the immediate pack (such as a blister, strip, bottle or vial), a package leaflet and a dispensing label added by the pharmacy. The outer carton usually carries more product and manufacturer details; a small blister may show only the information that fits. The patient information leaflet explains how to use the medicine and important safety information. The pharmacy label gives directions for the particular patient and prescription.

Read the carton and leaflet when you first receive a medicine, then check the immediate pack before each use. If the pharmacy directions differ from what you expected, pause and confirm them with the dispensing pharmacist or prescriber.

2. Check the brand name and generic name

The brand name identifies the marketed product. The generic name (active ingredient or INN) identifies the medicine substance responsible for its intended action. In Bangladesh, DGDA packaging guidance places the generic name with the brand name and requires key product details on packaging.

This matters because products with similar brand names may contain different ingredients, while different brands may contain the same active ingredient. Check the ingredient list on every product—especially when using a cold, pain or fever medicine alongside a prescription. Taking two products with the same active ingredient can unintentionally duplicate a dose. If you are unsure whether two products are equivalent, ask a pharmacist.

3. Understand strength, dosage form and dose

Strength tells you how much active ingredient is in a unit or measured quantity. Examples include 500 mg per tablet or 125 mg per 5 mL of liquid. Dosage form describes how the medicine is supplied—tablet, capsule, oral liquid, cream, inhaler, injection, eye drop or another form. Dose is the amount a person is instructed to take at one time.

These terms are not interchangeable. A 500 mg tablet does not automatically mean every patient should take one tablet. The correct dose depends on the prescription, product information, age, condition and other patient factors. For liquids, read the concentration carefully: 5 mL is a volume, not a universal dose. Use the supplied oral syringe or measuring cup, not a kitchen spoon.

For children, never calculate a dose by copying an adult instruction. Confirm the child's age/weight-based directions and the exact liquid concentration with a pharmacist or prescriber.

4. Follow the directions: how much, how often, by which route and for how long

Look for the amount per dose, interval or frequency, route of administration and duration. Terms such as “once daily”, “twice daily”, “after food” or “as needed” have specific meanings; if an abbreviation is unfamiliar, ask the pharmacist to explain it. Do not assume that a medicine is taken by mouth just because it is a tablet-like product.

Pay attention to special instructions printed on the pack or leaflet, such as “shake well before use”, “do not chew”, “do not crush”, “for external use only” or “read the leaflet before use”. Modified-release and enteric-coated products may be damaged if crushed or split. Do not change the route, formulation or schedule without professional advice.

For a missed dose, follow the leaflet or ask a pharmacist. Do not double the next dose unless a healthcare professional specifically tells you to.

5. Read warnings and special precautions

Warnings may explain who should avoid the medicine, when medical advice is needed, possible side effects, interactions and activities that require caution. Check for instructions related to allergies, pregnancy or breastfeeding, kidney or liver disease, alcohol, driving, food and other medicines. “Prescription only” means the medicine should be used under the appropriate prescription and professional supervision.

Some medicines can cause drowsiness or affect alertness. Some must be stored or handled carefully. For eye, ear or nasal drops, confirm the correct product and route; for creams and lotions, check whether the label says “for external use only”. If the warning is unclear or seems inconsistent with your prescription, ask before using the product.

6. Check MFG, EXP and the batch number

MFG or “Mfg. Date” is the manufacturing date. EXP or “Expiry Date” is the date after which the manufacturer does not guarantee the product's stated quality, safety and performance under the labelled conditions. Do not confuse the two. A medicine can be within its expiry period but still be unsuitable if it has been stored incorrectly or the pack is damaged.

The Bangladesh DGDA's 2023 labelling guideline states that when an expiry date is printed using only month and year, it means the last day of that month. For example, “EXP 08/2027” means the end of August 2027 under that guideline. Always follow the exact product information and do not use a medicine beyond its expiry date.

The batch number (lot number) identifies a manufacturing batch and is useful if a product quality concern or recall needs to be investigated. Keep the batch details and packaging if you need to report a problem.

7. Check storage, pack integrity and after-opening instructions

Follow the storage conditions printed on the label or leaflet. “Store below” a stated temperature is not the same as “refrigerate”; do not place a medicine in the refrigerator unless its instructions require it. Protect products from heat, moisture or light when directed, and keep all medicines out of the sight and reach of children.

Some liquids, eye drops, creams or reconstituted suspensions have a limited usable period after opening or preparation. The expiry date on the unopened pack may not be the same as the in-use expiry after opening or reconstitution. Record the opening or preparation date when instructed and follow the stated discard date. For dry powders that need water added, use the exact preparation method and water quantity in the leaflet or from the pharmacist.

Before use, check that the seal and packaging are intact and that the medicine looks as expected. Do not use a pack with suspicious damage, altered printing, missing dates, an unreadable label, unusual colour, smell or appearance. Ask a pharmacist to check it and use the official DGDA channel for suspected product-quality concerns.

8. A simple medicine-label checklist

  • Correct patient and product: brand name plus generic/active ingredient
  • Correct strength, dosage form and route
  • Clear dose, frequency, timing and duration
  • Warnings, allergies and important interactions reviewed
  • MFG, EXP and batch number readable and appropriate
  • Storage and after-opening/reconstitution instructions understood
  • Packaging intact and medicine appearance as expected

If you cannot read the label, the instructions conflict, or the medicine looks different from what you normally receive, do not guess. Take the pack to a registered pharmacist or contact the prescriber.

Key takeaways

  • Strength is not the same as dose.
  • Check the generic ingredient to avoid accidentally taking the same medicine twice under different brand names.
  • Follow the exact route, directions, warnings and special handling instructions.
  • EXP is different from MFG; check batch number and pack integrity too.
  • Storage and in-use expiry after opening or reconstitution may differ from the unopened pack expiry.
  • Ask a pharmacist whenever the label or prescription is unclear.

Frequently asked questions

What information should I check first on a medicine label?

Check the brand and generic names, strength, dosage form, route, directions, warnings, expiry date, batch number and storage instructions. Also read the package leaflet and pharmacy directions.

What is the difference between strength and dose?

Strength is the amount of active ingredient in a tablet, capsule or measured volume. Dose is the amount a particular person is instructed to take at one time. A product's strength does not tell you the correct dose by itself.

What do MFG and EXP mean?

MFG means manufacturing date. EXP means expiry date. Under Bangladesh DGDA's 2023 labelling guideline, an expiry date printed as month and year refers to the last day of that month.

Can I use a medicine after opening if the expiry date has not passed?

Not always. Some products have a shorter in-use period after opening, dilution or reconstitution. Follow the leaflet or pharmacist's instructions and record the opening or preparation date when required.

Why should I check the generic name as well as the brand name?

Different brands can contain the same active ingredient, and similar brand names can contain different ingredients. Checking the generic name helps prevent accidental duplication and confusion.

What should I do if the label is damaged or the directions are unclear?

Do not guess or use an unreadable product. Keep the packaging and ask the dispensing pharmacist or prescriber to confirm the medicine and instructions before use.
