Pharmaceutical Regulatory Inspections: What Do Inspectors Check?

How regulators plan risk-based inspections, examine facilities and records, document findings and follow up corrective actions—with Bangladesh DGDA context.

Englishবাংলা

By Sayedul Abrar, pharmacist · Published 28 November 2026

The short answer: A pharmaceutical regulatory inspection is an official, evidence-based assessment by a regulator or authorised inspector. It checks whether a facility, process, record or supply-chain activity complies with applicable law and good practices—and whether weaknesses could put medicine quality or patients at risk.

1. What is a pharmaceutical regulatory inspection?

A regulatory inspection is an official examination conducted by a national regulatory authority or an inspector acting within an authorised mandate. Depending on the product and facility, it may assess a pharmaceutical factory, an active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) site, a laboratory, an importer, a warehouse, a distributor, a pharmacy or another point in the medicine supply chain.

The inspector does more than look around a building. They compare what actually happens with the applicable law, licence conditions, approved procedures and relevant good-practice requirements. Evidence may include direct observation, staff interviews, controlled documents, original laboratory data, electronic records, equipment logs, material status labels, batch histories and storage records.

A facility can look clean and still have serious weaknesses. For example, a room may appear tidy while its air-handling system is not performing as qualified, a balance is overdue for calibration, a critical electronic audit trail is not reviewed, or a batch record does not reflect what happened on the production floor. Inspection is designed to test the system behind the appearance.

2. Why do regulators inspect pharmaceutical facilities?

Medicines are manufactured and handled through processes that can introduce contamination, mix-ups, incorrect strength, unreliable testing, poor storage or falsified products. Regulators use inspections to assess whether establishments comply with applicable requirements and whether the controls are strong enough to protect patients.

WHO describes regulatory inspection as one part of a wider system for detecting and responding to substandard and falsified medical products. Inspections can examine physical premises, processes, company documents and information systems, quality management and risk management. They can also support investigations into complaints or suspected product problems.

Inspection is not the same as an internal audit. A company's own audit is part of its quality system and is intended to identify and correct weaknesses. A regulatory inspection is carried out under public authority and may contribute to licensing, regulatory decisions or enforcement. Both can be valuable, but their legal roles are different.

3. What types of regulatory inspections are there?

The name and procedure differ between authorities, but common inspection purposes include:

  • Pre-licensing or pre-authorisation inspection: assesses a proposed facility or activity before a licence, approval or other regulatory authorisation is granted. A regulator may also assess significant changes or a new dosage form, process or facility.
  • Routine or periodic inspection: checks whether a licensed establishment continues to comply. Modern inspection programmes increasingly use risk information to decide which sites need attention and how broad an inspection should be.
  • For-cause or investigative inspection: responds to a complaint, suspected defect, recall, data-integrity concern, serious deviation, unusual test result or other credible signal that needs investigation.
  • Follow-up inspection: checks whether promised corrective and preventive actions (CAPA) have been implemented and are effective, and whether the original risk has been reduced.
  • Supply-chain inspection: examines import, storage, transport, wholesale, distribution or dispensing activities. A medicine can lose quality after manufacture if it is stored, handled or transported incorrectly.

Not every inspection is a full review of every activity. The purpose, scope and legal basis should guide what is examined. Whether an inspection is announced or unannounced also depends on the applicable law, authority procedures and reason for the visit; one universal rule should not be assumed.

4. How are inspections planned using risk?

Risk-based inspection means that the regulator directs its limited time and expertise towards the facilities, products and activities where a failure could cause the greatest harm. It does not mean that low-risk sites are permanently exempt from oversight.

WHO's 2026 Technical Report Series 1067, Annex 9, recommends that inspection planning consider both product risk and the establishment's compliance history. Relevant factors can include previous inspection findings, complaints, recalls, quality-test results, suspected substandard or falsified incidents, changes to equipment or key personnel, site complexity, product type and potential patient harm. Sterile products, biological medicines and complex processes may require different expertise from a simple non-sterile operation.

The risk assessment can influence the inspection team, duration, scope and timing of follow-up. The authority should document its reasoning and use tools suited to the type of facility. WHO provides examples of risk-assessment tools; these are not a universal inspection calendar or proof that every national regulator uses the same scoring system.

5. What do inspectors actually check?

The scope depends on the site and the product. A manufacturing inspection may examine the following connected areas:

  • People and training: Are responsibilities clear? Do staff have suitable training, experience, hygiene practices and access controls for their duties?
  • Premises and utilities: Are rooms, flows, ventilation, water, environmental controls, cleaning and segregation suitable for the activities being performed?
  • Equipment and systems: Are equipment and instruments fit for purpose, maintained, calibrated or qualified as appropriate? Are computerised systems controlled and access restricted?
  • Materials and suppliers: Can the site identify, quarantine, sample, test, approve and trace raw materials and packaging components? Are supplier controls proportionate to risk?
  • Manufacturing, packaging and testing: Do actual operations follow approved instructions? Are process controls, line clearance, in-process checks, laboratory methods and batch records reliable?
  • Quality management and data: Are deviations, complaints, out-of-specification results, change control, validation, self-inspection, CAPA and management review handled effectively? Can original data and audit trails be reconstructed?
  • Storage and distribution: Are product status, stock rotation, security, temperature conditions, transport, returns and recall traceability controlled?

Inspectors may follow a batch or material backwards and forwards through the records: from receipt, sampling and testing to dispensing, manufacture, packaging, release and distribution. This tests whether the evidence is connected, complete and consistent—not merely whether a procedure exists on paper.

For more background, see the guides to Good Manufacturing Practices, Quality Assurance vs Quality Control and OOS investigations.

6. What happens during an inspection?

A typical inspection can be understood in six broad stages. This is an educational overview, not a claim that every authority uses an identical checklist.

  1. Preparation: the authority defines the purpose and scope, reviews available information and assesses risk. The establishment may be asked to provide a site master file or selected documents where the applicable procedure requires it.
  2. Opening meeting: inspectors explain their identity, mandate, objectives, scope, practical arrangements and key contacts. The site should identify people who can provide accurate information.
  3. On-site examination: inspectors tour relevant areas, observe activities, interview personnel and compare actual practice with approved procedures. They may ask staff to explain a task rather than simply show a signed training record.
  4. Record and data review: the team examines documents, original records, electronic data, audit trails, equipment logs, batch records and investigation files relevant to the inspection.
  5. Sampling and close-out: where legally authorised and justified, samples may be collected using the prescribed procedure. Inspectors discuss observations and clarify factual points at the closing meeting.
  6. Report and follow-up: findings are documented in an inspection report. The establishment may be required to submit a response or CAPA plan, which the authority reviews under its own procedures.

Cooperation does not mean guessing, hiding a problem or creating records after the event. If information is not immediately available, it is better to say so, identify the record owner and provide a controlled, traceable response.

7. How are findings, inspection reports and CAPA handled?

A useful inspection finding describes what was observed, the evidence reviewed, the requirement or expected practice and the potential risk. It should be clear enough for the establishment to understand the gap and prepare a meaningful response.

WHO guidance gives critical, major and minor as examples of risk-based classifications for departures from good practices. These labels and their definitions are not automatically identical in every country or inspection programme. The authority's applicable rules and procedures determine the formal classification.

  • Critical: an example could be a serious failure that creates an immediate or substantial risk to patients or product integrity.
  • Major: an example could be a significant system failure or repeated weakness that may affect quality or indicates inadequate control.
  • Minor: an example could be an isolated departure with a more limited risk, while still requiring correction.

CAPA should go beyond a quick correction. A strong response normally identifies the affected products or activities, contains the immediate risk, investigates the underlying cause, assigns corrective and preventive actions, sets owners and due dates, and checks whether the actions work over time. A completed training session or revised SOP is not automatically proof that the root cause has been removed.

WHO's 2026 guidance recommends that regulators review CAPA responses and use the outcome when considering the site's continuing acceptability, risk rating and next inspection. The report and response should be handled within the timeframes and procedures set by the relevant authority.

8. Bangladesh: what does the DGDA legal framework say?

In Bangladesh, the Directorate General of Drug Administration (DGDA) is the national medicines regulatory authority. The Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 2023 (Act No. 29 of 2023) provides important statutory provisions relevant to good practices, inspector appointments, inspection powers, sampling and licensing decisions.

Section 17 states that establishments manufacturing or selling medicines must follow WHO Good Practices (GxP) guidelines in manufacturing, quality control, distribution, supply and storage. This is broader than a factory-only view of quality.

Section 47 provides for the appointment of suitably qualified inspectors. It also says a person with a financial interest in the production, import, export, sale or marketing of drugs or cosmetics cannot be appointed as an inspector under that provision.

Section 48 describes inspectors' powers within their assigned jurisdiction and, with the required permission, beyond it. The listed powers include inspecting manufacturing premises, plants, capacity, processes, testing and analysis equipment, records and registers; examining storage of imported raw and packaging materials; inspecting places where products are sold, stored or distributed; and collecting samples. The section also describes search, seizure, sealing and restrictions on removing evidence in specified circumstances. These are statutory powers with conditions—not a statement that every routine visit will involve search, seizure or sealing.

Section 50 sets out procedures for seizure and sample collection, including how samples are handled and distributed for testing and other purposes. Formal sampling matters because the identity, integrity and chain of custody of a sample can affect the reliability of later analysis.

Licensing decisions are a separate legal step. Section 18 allows the licensing authority to suspend or cancel a medicine licence on specified grounds, including failure to manufacture in accordance with GMP. It provides for written notice and an opportunity for the licence holder to respond before the relevant decision. Section 19 provides a review or appeal route within 30 working days for the specified suspension and production-stop orders. Therefore, an inspection observation should not be described as an automatic licence cancellation.

DGDA-hosted Quality Manual, NRA-QM-001 Version 03, effective March 2024, describes a quality-management framework for regulatory functions including inspection, assessment, testing, quality control, quality assurance, market surveillance and enforcement. Its regulatory-inspection scope includes assessment of manufacturing facilities for relevant licensing and GMP-related purposes. The exact requirements for an establishment must still be checked against the current Act, applicable rules, DGDA notices, licence conditions and product-specific directions.

WHO technical reports are international guidance, not automatically a substitute for Bangladesh law. WHO TRS 1067 (2026), Annex 9, is especially useful for understanding risk-based inspection and market surveillance across the supply chain; national legal effect depends on local adoption and applicable requirements.

9. How should a pharmaceutical company respond?

A sound response is factual, timely and focused on patient and product risk. A practical sequence is:

  • Preserve records, original data and relevant samples; do not backdate, overwrite or reconstruct evidence as if it were contemporaneous.
  • Assess whether a product, batch, process or distribution activity needs immediate containment under the applicable procedure.
  • Confirm the facts and identify the exact requirement or procedure that was not met.
  • Investigate the root cause rather than stopping at an individual error or a superficial explanation.
  • Define corrective and preventive actions with responsible owners, due dates, risk assessment and effectiveness checks.
  • Submit the response through the regulator's prescribed channel and maintain a traceable record of follow-up.

Companies should not treat an inspection as a presentation exercise. The strongest preparation is a quality system that is used every day, with accurate records, trained people, controlled changes and effective investigations.

10. Why should patients and healthcare professionals care?

Patients cannot personally verify a manufacturer's equipment qualification, laboratory data or distribution records. They depend on manufacturers, regulators, distributors and healthcare professionals to maintain quality throughout the medicine's life cycle. Regulatory inspections are one layer of that protection, alongside product testing, licensing, market surveillance, pharmacovigilance and recall systems.

An inspection or certificate is not a guarantee that every future batch is perfect. Quality must be maintained continuously, and product problems can arise after manufacture during storage or distribution. Healthcare professionals should use authorised reporting routes for suspected defects, unusual product appearance, damaged packaging or other concerns. Patients should ask a pharmacist or qualified healthcare professional when they notice a possible medicine-quality issue and should not use a visibly damaged or suspicious product without appropriate advice.

Key takeaways

  • Regulatory inspections are official assessments of compliance, not simply visual factory visits.
  • They may cover manufacturing, laboratories, storage, distribution and other regulated activities.
  • Risk, previous findings, complaints, product complexity and patient impact can shape inspection planning.
  • Inspectors review people, premises, equipment, materials, processes, records, data and quality systems.
  • Findings should be documented and addressed through evidence-based CAPA and follow-up.
  • Bangladesh's Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 2023 sets out GxP duties, inspector powers, sample procedures and licensing processes.
  • Inspection is one part of continuous regulatory oversight; it does not guarantee every future batch.

Frequently asked questions

Do regulatory inspections happen only at pharmaceutical factories?

No. Depending on the authority's mandate and the risk, inspections may cover manufacturers, API sites, laboratories, importers, warehouses, distributors, pharmacies and other facilities in the supply chain.

Is every regulatory inspection announced in advance?

Not necessarily. Notice arrangements depend on the applicable law, authority procedure and inspection purpose. Do not assume that every regulator or every type of inspection follows the same announcement rule.

What is the difference between a regulatory inspection and an internal audit?

A regulatory inspection is conducted under public authority to assess legal and regulatory compliance. An internal audit is organised by the company as part of its own quality system. They can examine similar evidence but have different mandates and consequences.

What does a critical inspection finding mean?

It generally describes a serious departure that may create substantial risk to patients or product integrity. Formal definitions and classification systems vary by regulator, so the applicable authority's rules must be followed.

Can DGDA inspectors collect medicine samples in Bangladesh?

Yes. Section 48 of the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 2023 includes sample-collection powers. Section 50 sets out relevant procedures for handling and distributing samples. The statutory conditions and prescribed procedure apply.

Does a GMP certificate guarantee that every batch is safe?

No. A certificate or inspection outcome relates to a defined scope and point in time. Manufacturers must continue to control each process and batch, and regulators may conduct further inspections or market surveillance.

What should a company do after receiving inspection observations?

Preserve the evidence, assess immediate product risk, investigate root causes, prepare proportionate CAPA with owners and due dates, check effectiveness and respond through the regulator's prescribed process.
