Immediate-Release vs Sustained-Release vs Enteric-Coated Medicines

Learn how medicine release designs differ and why modified-release tablets should not be crushed without advice.

Englishবাংলা

By Sayedul Abrar, pharmacist · 2 December 2026

In simple terms: Immediate-release medicines release the drug without a special delay or prolonged-release design. Sustained/extended-release products release it over a longer period. Enteric-coated products delay release until they pass through the stomach. These designs are different—and changing the tablet can change how it works.

1. Immediate-release (IR): conventional release

An immediate-release (IR) medicine has no special formulation feature intended to delay or prolong the release of its active ingredient. After administration, the dosage form still has to disintegrate or dissolve (where applicable), and the drug must be absorbed before it can produce a systemic effect. So immediate-release does not mean instant action.

For example, an ordinary immediate-release tablet may release its drug relatively promptly after it reaches the gastrointestinal tract. How quickly a person feels an effect still depends on the drug, the dose, food, the route, absorption and the condition being treated.

2. Sustained-release, extended-release and prolonged-release

These products are designed to release the active ingredient more slowly or over a longer period than a conventional immediate-release product given by the same route. The European Medicines Agency (EMA) uses prolonged-release for modified-release products that release more slowly than the conventional form; extended-release is used as an equivalent term in its quality guideline. “Sustained-release” is also commonly used in product names and clinical communication.

A longer release profile may help maintain drug exposure for longer or reduce how often a dose is taken. It does not automatically mean the medicine is stronger, safer, or suitable for every patient. The release pattern is created by product-specific formulation and manufacturing design.

How can a formulation release medicine slowly?

  • Matrix systems: the drug is dispersed within a material that controls how quickly liquid enters and the drug diffuses out or the matrix erodes.
  • Coated units: a membrane around a tablet, granule or pellet controls the movement of the drug out of the unit.
  • Multiparticulate systems: a capsule or tablet contains many coated pellets or beads that release the drug according to their design.

These are examples of formulation approaches, not a way to identify a product by appearance. Different technologies can produce similar-looking tablets.

3. Delayed-release and enteric-coated medicines

A delayed-release product is designed to postpone the start of drug release for a defined period or until a particular condition is met. An enteric coating is a common pH-dependent approach: the coating resists the acidic environment of the stomach and dissolves more readily at the higher pH found further along the gastrointestinal tract.

This can be useful when an active ingredient is unstable in gastric acid, or when delaying release is part of the intended product design. It does not mean every enteric-coated medicine is designed for the same reason, nor does the coating by itself guarantee that all drug absorption occurs only in the intestine.

Important: Enteric-coated and sustained-release are not synonyms. Enteric coating mainly changes where or when release begins; sustained/extended-release changes how slowly the drug is released over time. A product can have more than one release feature, so always check its own approved information.

5. What do the abbreviations on a medicine pack mean?

You may see abbreviations such as IR, SR, CR, ER, XR, XL, MR, DR, EC or GR. They can indicate a release characteristic, but abbreviations and naming conventions are not perfectly uniform across manufacturers or countries. The same letters should not be treated as a universal technical definition. Check the full product name, package insert and patient leaflet rather than relying on the abbreviation alone.

6. Can you split, crush, chew or open these medicines?

Do not crush or chew a modified-release tablet or an enteric-coated tablet unless the specific product information explicitly says it is safe. Crushing a prolonged-release product can destroy its release control and may cause the drug to be released too quickly. Crushing a gastro-resistant tablet can damage the coating and expose the drug to stomach acid or release it at the wrong site.

There are product-specific exceptions. Some scored modified-release tablets may be split, and some capsules containing coated pellets may be opened if the pellets are swallowed intact or used exactly as the leaflet directs. The pellets must not automatically be crushed or chewed. Instructions can differ even between brands containing the same active ingredient.

If swallowing is difficult, ask a pharmacist or prescriber to check the exact brand and formulation. A suitable liquid, dispersible or alternative formulation may be available. Do not change the dose or dosing interval yourself.

7. Key takeaways

  • Immediate-release means no special release delay or prolongation; it does not mean the medicine works instantly.
  • Sustained-, extended- and prolonged-release products are designed to release medicine over a longer period.
  • Delayed-release products postpone the start of release; enteric coating is one common approach.
  • Release abbreviations are not a substitute for checking the product-specific leaflet.
  • Never crush, chew, split or open a medicine based only on its appearance. Confirm the exact product instructions first.

Remember

Release design is part of the medicine—not just the shape of the tablet. Changing that design can change where, when or how quickly the active ingredient is released.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between immediate-release and sustained-release medicine?

Immediate-release products have no special design to prolong release. Sustained- or extended-release products are formulated to release the active ingredient over a longer period. Immediate-release does not mean instant action.

Is enteric-coated the same as sustained-release?

No. Enteric coating delays release until the dosage form reaches a suitable environment beyond the stomach. Sustained-release controls the release rate over a longer period. They describe different formulation goals.

Does enteric coating mean a medicine is absorbed only in the intestine?

Not necessarily. The coating is designed to resist stomach conditions and delay release, but the site and extent of absorption depend on the active ingredient and the complete formulation.

Can I split a sustained-release tablet?

Do not assume it is safe. Some specific scored products may be split, while others must remain intact. Check the exact product leaflet or ask a pharmacist before splitting, crushing or chewing it.

Can I open a capsule that contains modified-release pellets?

Some products allow the capsule shell to be opened while the pellets remain intact. Others do not. Follow the product-specific instructions; never crush or chew the pellets unless the approved information explicitly permits it.
