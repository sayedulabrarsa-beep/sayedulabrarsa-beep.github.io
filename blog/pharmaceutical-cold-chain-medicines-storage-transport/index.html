Pharmaceutical Cold Chain: How Are Temperature-Sensitive Medicines Stored and Transported?

A practical guide to product-specific storage, monitoring, packaging, transport and temperature-excursion decisions.

Englishবাংলা

By Sayedul Abrar · A Grade Pharmacist · Published 27 November 2026

A medicine may leave a manufacturing site within specification, yet face heat, freezing, delays or a power failure before it reaches a patient. For some products, the journey must remain within defined temperature conditions from manufacture until use. This connected system is called the pharmaceutical cold chain.

The central rule is simple: protect each product within the conditions established by its manufacturer and supported by stability data. But a cold chain is more than a refrigerator, and 2–8°C is not a universal rule for all medicines.

In short: A pharmaceutical cold chain is a controlled, monitored and documented journey for medicines that are sensitive to time and temperature. The product label determines the required conditions. Suitable storage, qualified packaging, trained staff and reliable temperature records help protect quality.

1. What is a pharmaceutical cold chain?

A time- and temperature-sensitive pharmaceutical product is a medicine or related health product that may degrade if it is not kept within its predefined environmental conditions and time limits. The World Health Organization (WHO) describes a model approach for storing and transporting these products in TRS 961, Annex 9.

In everyday use, the term cold chain often describes the temperature-controlled supply chain that links the manufacturer, warehouse, transport provider, distributor, pharmacy or hospital and finally the patient. Temperature control must continue at each hand-off. A well-maintained refrigerator at the destination cannot correct an uncontrolled journey that happened earlier.

Storage and transport ranges do not always have to be identical. They are determined by the product manufacturer using stability data and the approved product information. Some products need refrigeration; others need frozen or deep-frozen conditions; many medicines do not need refrigeration at all.

2. Which medicines need temperature control?

Examples can include vaccines, insulin, some monoclonal antibodies and other biological medicines, selected fertility or hormone products, and certain preparations after reconstitution. The exact requirement differs by brand, formulation, presentation and stage of use. A vial that must be refrigerated before opening may have a different, time-limited in-use allowance after first use or preparation.

Do not assume that every injection, vaccine or biologic has the same storage requirement. One product may be refrigerated, another frozen, and another labelled for controlled room-temperature storage. The carton, package insert and current manufacturer information are the starting points.

WHO's 2020 guidance on good storage and distribution practices (TRS 1025, Annex 7) gives recommended limits for descriptive storage terms. These are reference limits, not a replacement for the conditions printed on a specific product.

Read the exact label. WHO's descriptive limits help explain storage language. The manufacturer's approved label and applicable national requirements determine what a particular product needs. Never move a medicine into a refrigerator simply because it is an injection.

3. Why does temperature matter?

Temperature can change the rate of chemical reactions. Excess heat may accelerate degradation, oxidation or hydrolysis and can reduce the amount of active medicine available. In protein-based medicines, changes in temperature may affect the protein's structure or encourage aggregation. Some products are also sensitive to freezing, light, moisture or repeated freeze–thaw cycles.

Freezing is not automatically safer than warming. A product labelled “refrigerate — do not freeze” can be damaged if it touches a frozen coolant pack or a cold spot in a refrigerator. For some vaccines and biological products, freezing can compromise quality even when the product later thaws and looks normal.

A medicine may show no obvious change in colour, smell or appearance after a temperature event. Visual inspection alone cannot prove that potency has been retained. Quality decisions must be supported by product-specific stability information, not by appearance or guesswork.

What does the research say? A 2023 Cochrane review of thermal stability and storage of human insulin included 17 studies, most of them laboratory studies, with limited clinical evidence. The findings are product- and condition-specific; they do not create a general permission to store every insulin product outside its label. A 2019 study using temperature sensors in domestic refrigerators also documented out-of-range exposures, including temperatures below freezing. That study measured temperature exposure, not the potency of every insulin dose. These examples show why real monitoring and product-specific advice matter.

4. How does the cold chain work?

The cold chain is a sequence of linked controls, not a single piece of equipment. It begins with the manufacturer's approved storage conditions and continues through warehousing, packaging, transport, receipt, local storage and the final point of use.

  • At the manufacturer: product stability data and approved labelling define the required conditions and any permitted in-use period.
  • At the warehouse: suitable rooms, refrigerators or freezers are qualified and monitored. Temperature mapping helps identify warmer and colder locations.
  • During packaging: the shipper, insulation and coolant arrangement are selected for the product, route, season and expected transit time.
  • During transport: the shipment is protected from avoidable delays and monitored with an appropriate temperature device. Active systems use powered refrigeration; passive systems use insulated packaging and conditioned coolants.
  • At receipt: trained staff check the shipment, review the logger record and transfer the product to suitable storage. A shipment with a suspected excursion should be held for assessment rather than automatically released.
  • At the pharmacy or hospital: storage conditions, stock rotation, temperature records and staff responsibilities continue until the product is dispensed or administered.

A passive shipper is not simply a box with ice packs. Poorly conditioned or incorrectly placed packs can create freezing points next to the product. The pack-out must be designed and tested so the medicine remains within its labelled limits for the intended journey, including realistic delays. WHO's technical supplements in TRS 992, Annex 5 provide additional practical material for temperature-sensitive distribution systems.

5. How is temperature monitored?

A thermometer gives a reading at one moment. A digital data logger records temperature over time, which is especially useful during transport or when a refrigerator is not continuously observed. Depending on the product and system, facilities may also use calibrated temperature-monitoring devices, alarms and documented minimum–maximum checks.

Monitoring works only when equipment is suitable and correctly placed. Temperature mapping identifies locations that may be warmer or colder than the rest of a room or unit. Sensors are then positioned according to the qualification results and the monitoring plan. Calibration, maintenance, alarm response and record review are part of the control system.

Facilities should also plan for power failure, equipment breakdown, severe weather, transport delays and staff absence. A backup location or transfer process is useful only if it can maintain the required conditions and the team knows how to activate it. A temperature log that nobody reviews is not an effective control.

6. What happens after a temperature excursion?

A temperature excursion occurs when a product is exposed outside its specified storage or transport range. It can be an event above the upper limit or below the lower limit, and it may happen in a warehouse, vehicle, refrigerator or temporary storage area.

An excursion does not automatically prove that every affected pack has become unusable. It also does not mean the product is safe simply because the event was short. The decision depends on the exact product, temperature reached, duration, cumulative exposure, packaging, previous events and the stability evidence available for that product.

A sensible response is to:

  1. Hold and identify the affected stock. Segregate it and label it according to the site's procedure so it is not accidentally supplied or used.
  2. Maintain the labelled storage condition. Do not deliberately warm, freeze or repeatedly move the product while waiting for advice.
  3. Preserve the evidence. Record the product and batch, the date and location, the start and end time, minimum and maximum temperatures, logger trace, route, packaging and any earlier excursions.
  4. Notify the responsible quality team and manufacturer. Follow the applicable company procedure and relevant regulatory or public-health instructions.
  5. Assess the product using its own stability evidence. The manufacturer or quality unit may need to evaluate the temperature profile, duration and cumulative exposure. A mathematical measure such as mean kinetic temperature may help for some suitable products, but it is not a universal substitute for the actual trace or product-specific data.
  6. Document the final decision. Only an authorized quality function or relevant authority should determine whether the stock can be released, returned, recalled or rejected.

A 2026 regulatory and stability-science review similarly emphasizes a documented, product-specific assessment of temperature-compromised products. The practical lesson is not “always discard” or “always use”; it is “hold, investigate and decide with evidence.”

7. What is the Bangladesh context?

Bangladesh's Directorate General of Drug Administration (DGDA) has a 2024 National Guideline on Accreditation of Model Pharmacy and Model Medicine Shop. Its assessment framework includes checks on preserving temperature-sensitive medicines and monitoring storage temperature; the Model Pharmacy framework also includes a pharmacy-grade refrigerator.

The guideline summarizes section 17.1 of the Drugs and Cosmetics Act 2023, which advises producers and sellers to follow WHO good-practice guidance in production, quality control, distribution, supply and storage. This is important context for medicine quality, but it should not be misread as a rule that every medicine must be stored at 2–8°C. The correct conditions remain product-specific, and the current applicable DGDA requirements must be followed.

In a warm climate, long journeys, border or airport delays and power interruptions can challenge temperature control. Importers, distributors, hospitals and pharmacies therefore need suitable equipment, qualified packaging, temperature records, backup arrangements and trained staff. The WHO model guidance is a technical reference; it does not replace Bangladesh's laws or product-specific approvals.

8. What should pharmacists and patients do?

For pharmacists and healthcare facilities

  • Read the exact storage statement for each product. Do not refrigerate every injection or biologic by default.
  • Use suitable, monitored storage equipment and follow the site's temperature-recording and alarm-response procedures.
  • Keep products in their required packaging when the label says so, and protect them from light or moisture when specified.
  • Use qualified transport containers and follow the manufacturer's pack-out instructions. Avoid direct contact between medicines and frozen coolant packs unless the validated design specifically allows it.
  • Check the in-use period after opening or reconstitution; it may differ from the unopened shelf-life storage condition.

For patients and caregivers

  • Follow the storage instructions on the carton and leaflet. A medicine should not be placed in a household refrigerator unless its product information says to do so.
  • Do not freeze a medicine labelled “refrigerate — do not freeze”, and do not leave temperature-sensitive products in a parked vehicle or direct sunlight.
  • Keep the original packaging and ask the pharmacist how to transport the medicine home, especially during hot weather or a long journey.
  • If a product has been exposed to heat or freezing, contact the dispensing pharmacy, clinic or manufacturer before using it. Do not judge its quality by appearance alone.
  • Do not stop or change essential treatment without advice from the treating healthcare professional.

9. Key takeaways

  • The cold chain is a monitored system from manufacturer to patient, not simply a refrigerator.
  • Storage limits are product-specific. 2–8°C is common for some products, not universal for all medicines.
  • Both heat and freezing can damage sensitive products; a medicine may look normal after an excursion.
  • Qualified packaging, calibrated monitoring, temperature records, trained staff and backup plans protect product quality.
  • After an excursion, hold the stock, preserve the evidence and obtain a documented, stability-based quality decision.

10. Frequently asked questions

Do all medicines need to be stored at 2–8°C?

No. Some products require refrigeration, some need frozen or deep-frozen storage, and many are labelled for controlled room-temperature conditions. Follow the exact product label.

Does every biologic medicine need refrigeration?

No. Biologic products have different formulations and stability profiles. Some require refrigeration, some have frozen or other conditions, and some allow a defined period at room temperature. Check the exact product information.

Can a medicine be used after a temperature excursion?

Sometimes a product may remain suitable, but this must be established from the actual temperature record and product-specific stability evidence. Keep the stock segregated and obtain a documented decision from the responsible quality unit and manufacturer or relevant authority before release or use.

Does a refrigerator guarantee correct medicine storage?

No. The equipment must be suitable, qualified and monitored. Temperature mapping, calibrated devices, alarms, records and contingency plans help identify hot or cold spots and power failures.

What is a temperature data logger?

It is an electronic device that records temperature over time. Its trace can show the duration and extent of exposure during storage or transport and support an investigation.

Can mean kinetic temperature decide whether an exposed product is safe?

Not by itself. Mean kinetic temperature may be useful for certain products when the method is appropriate, but it does not replace the actual temperature record, product-specific stability data or a documented quality assessment.

What does Bangladesh guidance say about cold-chain storage?

DGDA's 2024 Model Pharmacy and Model Medicine Shop accreditation guideline includes checks for preserving temperature-sensitive medicines, monitoring temperature and having a pharmacy-grade refrigerator in a Model Pharmacy. It does not set one universal temperature range for every medicine.
