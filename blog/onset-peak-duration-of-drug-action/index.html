Onset, Peak and Duration: When Does a Medicine Start and Stop Working?

How to read the time curve of a medicine: when it starts working, when it is strongest, how long it lasts, and why a slow start is not a reason to take more.

Englishবাংলা

By Sayedul Abrar, pharmacist · 28 November 2026

In simple terms: Onset is when a medicine first starts to work. Peak effect is when the medicine produces its greatest desired response. The highest blood concentration is a separate measure called Cmax, reached at Tmax. Duration is how long it keeps working. A medicine helps while its level stays between the minimum effective level and the level that causes toxic effects.

1. Three simple words

  • Onset of action: the time when the medicine first begins to take effect.
  • Peak effect: the time when the medicine produces its greatest desired response. Do not confuse this with peak concentration: the highest measured blood level is Cmax, and the time it occurs is Tmax. These may not occur at exactly the same time because the relationship between concentration and effect depends on the medicine.
  • Duration of action: the length of time the medicine produces its desired effect.

2. Reading the concentration–time curve

If you measure the drug level in the blood after a tablet and plot it against time, you get a curve. It rises as the drug is absorbed, reaches a peak blood concentration, and then falls as the body removes the drug (see Drug Half-Life).

In this simplified model, the curve is blood concentration, not the clinical effect itself. Below the minimum effective concentration (MEC), the desired response may be insufficient; above a concentration associated with toxicity, harm becomes more likely. The range between these limits is the therapeutic window. Onset is when the desired effect first becomes noticeable, and duration is how long that effect continues. For some medicines, the effect can lag behind blood concentration or persist after levels fall, so the two curves do not always match.

3. Narrow and wide windows

Some medicines have a wide gap between the effective level and the toxic level. Others have a narrow gap, so a small change in the level matters. Pharmacists use the therapeutic index to describe this safety margin. StatPearls names warfarin, lithium and theophylline as examples of medicines with a narrow therapeutic index; they need close monitoring by the treating team.

4. Why some medicines act in minutes and others in weeks

Route matters. A medicine given into a vein is delivered straight into the blood and acts faster than by other routes (MSD Manual). A medicine placed under the tongue can also act quickly; StatPearls describes sublingual nitroglycerin acting in about 2 minutes, with a short duration.

Form matters. Slow-release (SR, ER or CR) tablets are designed to release the drug over a longer time, reduce how often you take it, and limit ups and downs in the blood level. The trade-off is that they are not meant for a quick effect.

The medicine's job matters. Some medicines work only after the body slowly adapts. The NHS says antidepressants usually take 1 to 2 weeks to start having an effect and can take up to 8 weeks to work fully. A slow start does not always mean the medicine is not working.

5. Why you should not double a dose when it feels slow

If a medicine seems slow, it is tempting to take another tablet. This can push the level above the window. The NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service advises: never take a double dose to make up for a forgotten dose unless a prescriber has told you to, because doses taken too close together can increase the risk of side effects.

If a medicine does not seem to work, or wears off too early, talk to your doctor or pharmacist. They may change the timing, the form or the medicine itself.

6. Key takeaways

Onset, peak and duration describe the timing of a medicine's effect. They depend on the route, the form, the half-life and the way the medicine works.

  • Onset is when a medicine starts to work; peak is when its level is highest; duration is how long it works.
  • A medicine helps while its level stays inside the therapeutic window.
  • IV and under-the-tongue routes act fast; slow-release tablets act longer.
  • Some medicines, such as antidepressants, need weeks for full effect.
  • Never double a dose because it feels slow; ask your doctor or pharmacist.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between onset and peak?

Onset is when the medicine first starts to work. Peak is later, when the medicine reaches its highest level in the body.

What is the therapeutic window?

It is the range of drug levels between the minimum effective level and the level where toxic effects become likely. The medicine works best and safest inside this range.

Why does my medicine take so long to work?

It can depend on the route, the dosage form, how long the medicine takes to build up, and how it works. Some medicines, like antidepressants, can take several weeks for their full effect. Ask your pharmacist what to expect.

Can I take an extra tablet if the first one has not worked yet?

Do not take an extra dose on your own. Doses taken too close together can raise the risk of side effects. Follow the label or ask your doctor or pharmacist.

Does a slow-release tablet work faster if I crush it?

Do not crush or chew slow-release tablets unless the leaflet or your pharmacist says it is safe. Crushing can release the whole dose at once.
