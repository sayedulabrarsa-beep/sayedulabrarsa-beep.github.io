How to Read a Prescription: BD, TDS, OD and Common Abbreviations

BD, TDS, OD, 1+0+1 and the other short forms on a prescription, explained simply.

Englishবাংলা

By Sayedul Abrar, pharmacist · Published 14 November 2026

Quick answer: A prescription tells you which medicine to take, how much, how often, before or after food, and for how long. Common short forms: OD once a day, BD twice a day, TDS three times a day, QDS four times a day, HS at bedtime, AC before food, PC after food, PRN only when needed. In Bangladesh, 1+0+1 means one in the morning and one at night. If anything is unclear, ask your pharmacist. Never guess.

A common moment (illustrative, fictional)

Rina collects her father’s medicines from a pharmacy in Dhaka. The prescription says “Tab. … 20 mg 1+0+1 AC × 14 days” and “Syp. … 2 tsf TDS PC × 5 days”. She knows the medicine names, but not what 1+0+1, AC, TDS or tsf mean. This article explains each one, so you can check a prescription before you leave the pharmacy. Rina is an example, not a real patient.

The 8 parts of a prescription

The World Health Organization’s Guide to Good Prescribing lists what a prescription should contain: the prescriber’s name, address and phone number; the date; the medicine name and strength; the dosage form and total amount; instructions and warnings; the patient’s name, address and age; and the prescriber’s signature. In Bangladesh, the pad usually also shows the doctor’s degrees and BMDC registration number.

Start with the basics: is it your name, today’s date, and a doctor you saw? Then read each medicine line from left to right: form and name, strength, how much, how often, before or after food, and for how long.

What does Rx mean?

Rx (℞) is the traditional sign at the start of the medicine list. It comes from the Latin word recipe, meaning “take”. It is not a medicine name.

How often: OD, BD, TDS, QDS

Most frequency short forms come from Latin. Australian prescribing guidance accepts written bd, tds, qid, prn and stat, but recommends writing “once a day” or “daily” in full rather than OD.

“Three times a day” is often taken morning, afternoon and night, spread across waking hours. For some medicines the gap matters more. If you are not sure how many hours to leave between doses, ask your pharmacist.

What does 1+0+1 mean?

Many prescriptions in Bangladesh use a simple number pattern instead of Latin: morning + midday + night. Each number is how many tablets (or doses) to take at that time. It may be written in English or Bangla digits (১+০+১).

  • 1+0+1: one in the morning, none at midday, one at night (twice a day).
  • 1+1+1: one in the morning, at midday and at night (three times a day).
  • 0+0+1: one at night only.
  • ½+0+½: half a tablet in the morning and at night. Ask first whether your tablet can be split. Some, such as modified-release tablets, should not be broken.

The numbers are doses, not hours. The strength (for example 20 mg) is written separately, so check it against the pack.

Before or after food: AC, PC and “empty stomach”

AC (ante cibum) means before food. PC (post cibum) means after food. Doctors in Bangladesh often write this in Bangla instead: খাবারের আগে or খাবারের পরে. “Empty stomach” usually means about an hour before food or two hours after, but follow your own leaflet.

Timing can change how well some medicines work. For example, many proton pump inhibitors are taken before a meal (see when to take a PPI), and iron tablets have their own food rules (see how to take iron tablets properly).

For how long: “× 7 days”, “1 month” and “continue”

The duration is often written as “× 5 days”, “7 days”, “1 month”, or “continue” (চলবে). Continue means keep taking it until your doctor reviews it, not forever without a check-up. For antibiotics, finish the course as written unless your doctor says otherwise. My article on why you must complete an antibiotic course explains why.

Forms and routes: Tab, Cap, Syp, Inj, PO

The line usually starts with the dosage form: Tab. tablet, Cap. capsule, Syp. syrup, Susp. suspension (shake it before use), Inj. injection, Inh. inhaler, or drops. The route may follow: PO by mouth, IM into a muscle, IV into a vein.

For liquids, tsf means teaspoonful, usually 5 mL. The FDA notes that kitchen spoons vary widely in size, and ISMP advises never to use a household spoon. Use the cup or oral syringe that comes with the medicine.

The medicine name on the prescription may be a brand name. The pack also shows the generic name (the active ingredient). My guide to generic name vs brand name shows how to match them.

Abbreviations that cause mistakes

Some short forms are easy to misread. The Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP) keeps a list of error-prone abbreviations. A few that matter to patients:

This is not just theory. A study of 200 patients’ prescriptions at a tertiary hospital in Rajshahi found 35 medicine names unclear because of handwriting, dose strength missing for 279 medicines, and 31 medicines with an improper abbreviation. An audit at a garment medical centre in Dhaka found dose and duration written on only 66% and 70% of prescriptions. Both are older, single-site studies, so they do not describe every prescription in Bangladesh, but they show why checking matters.

How to check your prescription at the pharmacy

  1. Check your name, the date and the doctor’s details.
  2. Match each medicine name and strength with the pack you are given.
  3. Read how much, how often and for how long.
  4. Check before or after food.
  5. Ask about anything unclear before you leave, and look at the expiry date. My guide to expiry date vs manufacturing date shows how to read it.

If the words drug and medicine confuse you, see drug vs medicine.

Key takeaways

  • OD once a day, BD twice, TDS three times, QDS four times a day.
  • 1+0+1 means one in the morning and one at night.
  • AC is before food, PC is after food, HS is at bedtime, PRN or SOS is only when needed.
  • Measure liquids in mL with the device in the pack, not a kitchen spoon.
  • Never guess. Ask your pharmacist or doctor.

Frequently asked questions

What does BD mean on a prescription?

BD means twice a day. It comes from the Latin bis die. BID means the same thing. It is usually taken in the morning and in the evening or at night. If the gap between doses matters for your medicine, ask your pharmacist.

What does TDS mean on a prescription?

TDS means three times a day, from the Latin ter die sumendum. TID means the same. Three times a day does not always mean exactly every 8 hours, so ask your pharmacist if the timing matters for your medicine.

What does OD mean on a prescription?

OD usually means once a day. But OD is also the abbreviation for the right eye (oculus dexter), which is why ISMP advises writing “daily” instead. If OD is written next to eye drops, ask whether it means once a day or the right eye.

What does 1+0+1 mean on a prescription?

It shows how many tablets or doses to take in the morning, at midday and at night. 1+0+1 means one in the morning, none at midday and one at night, so twice a day. 1+1+1 means three times a day, and 0+0+1 means one at night only. The numbers are doses, not hours.

What is the difference between PRN and SOS?

Both mean “only when needed”, for example for pain or fever. PRN comes from pro re nata and SOS from si opus sit. Ask your doctor or pharmacist the most you can take in a day and how long to wait between doses.

What does “continue” or “চলবে” mean on a prescription?

It means keep taking the medicine until your doctor reviews it. It does not mean forever without a check-up. Do not stop on your own, and ask when you should go back for a review.

What should I do if I cannot read the doctor’s handwriting?

Do not guess. Ask the pharmacist to read it with you. If the pharmacist is not sure either, call the doctor’s chamber. A Bangladesh hospital study found medicine names that were unclear because of handwriting, so checking is sensible, not rude.

Is 1 tsf (teaspoonful) the same as 5 mL?

On a prescription, one teaspoonful (tsf) is usually meant as 5 mL. Kitchen teaspoons vary in size, so measure with the cup or oral syringe that comes with the medicine, not a household spoon (FDA, ISMP).
