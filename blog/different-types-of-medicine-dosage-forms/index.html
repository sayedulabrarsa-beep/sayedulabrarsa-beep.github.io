Different Types of Medicine Dosage Forms: Tablets, Capsules, Liquids and More

A practical guide to common dosage forms and why medicines are made in different forms.

Englishবাংলা

By Sayedul Abrar, pharmacist · 30 November 2026

In simple terms: A practical guide to common dosage forms and why medicines are made in different forms.

1. What is a dosage form?

A dosage form is the physical form in which a medicine is supplied—for example, a tablet, capsule, oral liquid, cream, eye drop, inhaler or injection. It combines the active pharmaceutical ingredient with other ingredients (excipients) so the medicine can be manufactured, stored and used in a consistent way. A dosage form describes the product; the route of administration describes how it enters or is applied to the body. The two are related, but they are not the same.

2. Solid oral forms

Tablets are solid units made by compressing powders or granules. They may be swallowed whole, chewed, dispersed in water, dissolved before use, or designed to release medicine in a particular way. Examples include conventional, chewable, dispersible, effervescent, orodispersible, buccal and sublingual tablets. Capsules contain powders, granules, pellets or liquids inside a shell; some are hard-shell and others soft-shell. A capsule may contain modified-release pellets, so opening it does not automatically make its contents safe to crush or chew. These forms are not automatically interchangeable, even when the active ingredient and strength look similar.

3. Liquid forms

In a solution, the drug is dissolved in the liquid. In a suspension, fine drug particles are dispersed through the liquid and the bottle may need shaking exactly as the label instructs. Emulsions contain one liquid dispersed in another. Syrups, oral solutions, suspensions, drops, powders and granules for reconstitution can be useful when swallowing solid forms is difficult. Measure liquid medicines with the supplied oral syringe, cup or measuring device—not a household spoon—and follow any storage or after-opening instructions.

4. Topical and inhaled forms

Creams, ointments, gels, lotions, pastes and medicated foams are applied to the skin. Many act locally, while a transdermal patch is designed to deliver medicine through the skin into the bloodstream over time. Inhalers deliver medicine to the airways as an aerosol or powder; nebulisers turn a suitable liquid into a mist. Correct device technique and cleaning instructions matter. Eye drops and eye ointments are ophthalmic products; ear drops are otic products; nasal drops and sprays are used in the nose. Do not use one product in the eye, ear or nose unless its label specifically says it is intended for that site.

5. Why does the form matter?

Other dosage forms include injections and infusions (for example, intravenous, intramuscular or subcutaneous preparations), rectal suppositories and enemas, and vaginal tablets, pessaries or creams. Injectable products must meet the appropriate sterility and quality requirements. The dosage form can influence where a medicine is released, how quickly it becomes available, its stability, convenience and suitability for a patient. A formulation is selected according to the drug's properties and the intended site and pattern of delivery—not simply personal preference.

6. Important safety points

Do not assume every tablet can be crushed or split, or every capsule can be opened. Crushing an enteric-coated or modified-release product can change how the medicine is released, its effectiveness or its safety. Some capsules can be opened only when their product-specific instructions allow it; pellets or granules inside may still need to remain intact. Check the patient information leaflet or ask a pharmacist before altering any medicine.

Also check the intended route, dose, measuring device, storage directions and expiry after reconstitution or opening. Never use an oral liquid by injection, or a skin preparation in the eye.

7. Key takeaways

Dosage forms are designed for particular routes, sites and release patterns. Read the label, measure liquids accurately and ask a pharmacist before altering a tablet or capsule.

Frequently asked questions

What is a medicine dosage form?

A dosage form is the physical form in which a medicine is supplied, such as a tablet, capsule, oral liquid, cream, inhaler or injection.

Why are medicines made in different dosage forms?

Dosage forms are designed for different routes, release patterns, stability needs and patient requirements. The same active ingredient may be available in more than one form.

Can I crush or split any tablet?

No. Some modified-release, enteric-coated or otherwise specialised tablets must not be crushed or split. Check the product-specific instructions and ask a pharmacist before altering a medicine.

Can I open a capsule and take the contents?

Not always. Some capsules contain coated pellets or granules that must not be chewed or crushed. Whether a capsule can be opened depends on the exact product; ask a pharmacist first.

What is the difference between a solution and a suspension?

A solution has the medicine dissolved in the liquid. A suspension has small particles dispersed through the liquid and may need shaking as directed on the label.
