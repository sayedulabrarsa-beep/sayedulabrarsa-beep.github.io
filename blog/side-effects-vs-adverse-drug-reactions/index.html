Side Effects vs Adverse Drug Reactions: What Is the Difference?

Learn the difference between side effects, adverse drug reactions, serious reactions and what to do.

Englishবাংলা

By Sayedul Abrar, pharmacist · 9 December 2026

In simple terms: A side effect is an unintended effect of a medicine. An adverse drug reaction (ADR) is a harmful, unintended response for which a link to the medicine is suspected. An adverse event may happen during treatment even when the medicine did not cause it.

1. What is a side effect?

A side effect is an unintended effect that occurs alongside a medicine's intended therapeutic effect. It may be predictable from the medicine's pharmacological action and can range from mild discomfort to a clinically important problem. Some side effects are dose-related; others depend on the medicine, the person, other medicines, food, or an underlying condition.

For example, a medicine that causes drowsiness may make a person sleepy even when it is being used correctly. Nausea, dry mouth and dizziness are other possible unwanted effects with some medicines. These examples are not universal: the likelihood and importance of an effect depend on the exact product and patient.

2. What is an adverse drug reaction (ADR)?

The Directorate General of Drug Administration (DGDA) in Bangladesh defines an adverse drug reaction (ADR) as a harmful (noxious) and unintended response to a medicine that occurs at doses normally used in humans for prevention, diagnosis, treatment or modification of physiological function. In pharmacovigilance, the term is used when a causal relationship with the medicine is suspected—not simply because a symptom happened at the same time.

In everyday conversation, people often use side effect and ADR as if they mean exactly the same thing. Their meanings overlap, but the formal terms help healthcare professionals describe, assess and report medicine-safety concerns more precisely. A side effect is a broad term for an unintended effect and may be mild or harmful. ADR is the formal term for a harmful, unintended response for which a medicine-related cause is suspected.

3. Adverse event (AE) vs ADR: why the difference matters

An adverse event (AE) is an unfavourable medical occurrence during treatment with a medicine or other health product. It does not automatically mean the product caused it. An ADR is a suspected harmful response with a possible causal link to the medicine.

This distinction is important. A person may develop a fever while taking a medicine because of an infection, the underlying illness or another cause. The event should be assessed rather than automatically labelled as an ADR. Equally, a suspected reaction should not be ignored just because the cause has not yet been confirmed.

4. Are all adverse reactions allergies?

No. A medicine allergy is only one possible type of adverse reaction. An allergic reaction involves the immune system. Other reactions may be related to a medicine's expected pharmacological action, dose, interaction with another product, organ function or individual susceptibility. For example, drowsiness from a sedating medicine is not automatically an allergy.

Do not add a medicine to an allergy list based only on a vague or unassessed symptom. Tell a healthcare professional what happened, when it began, how severe it was and which medicine was involved. Accurate documentation helps prevent both repeat exposure to a genuine allergen and unnecessary avoidance of useful medicines.

5. Serious vs severe: are they the same?

These words describe different things. Severe usually describes the intensity of a symptom. Serious is a pharmacovigilance and regulatory classification based on the outcome. Under the Bangladesh national guideline, a serious adverse event or reaction includes an occurrence that results in death, is life-threatening, requires inpatient hospitalisation or prolongs hospitalisation, or causes persistent or significant disability/incapacity.

A symptom can feel severe without meeting a formal seriousness criterion, and an event can be serious even if the initial symptom does not seem dramatic. Any worrying or rapidly worsening symptom deserves prompt professional assessment; do not try to classify it yourself.

6. Warning signs: when should you seek urgent help?

Seek emergency medical care if a medicine is followed by difficulty breathing, swelling of the tongue or throat, collapse or fainting, or a rapidly worsening reaction with widespread blistering or peeling skin. These can indicate a life-threatening reaction. Do not wait for a routine appointment.

For other new, persistent or concerning symptoms—such as a rash, unusual bleeding, severe vomiting, marked dizziness or yellowing of the skin or eyes—contact a doctor or pharmacist promptly. The urgency depends on the symptom, the medicine and the person's condition. This article cannot determine whether a particular symptom is caused by a medicine.

7. What should you do if you suspect a reaction?

  • Prioritise safety. For emergency symptoms, seek emergency care immediately.
  • Do not take an extra dose, deliberately repeat a suspected reaction, or restart a medicine after a serious reaction without professional advice.
  • Contact the prescriber or pharmacist promptly for guidance about the next dose and whether the medicine should be changed. Do not stop essential long-term treatment on your own unless emergency clinicians instruct you to do so.
  • Keep the medicine pack, leaflet or a clear photo of the label. Record the brand and generic name, strength, dose, route and when the medicine was started.
  • Write down what happened, when it began, how long it lasted, any treatment received and whether other medicines, supplements or foods were involved.
  • Share your full medicine list, including non-prescription products and herbal supplements. A healthcare professional can consider other possible causes and assess whether the event is related.

8. How are suspected reactions reported in Bangladesh?

Pharmacovigilance is the ongoing work of detecting, assessing, understanding and preventing medicine-related harm. It helps identify safety signals that may not have been clear before a medicine was used by many people. A report does not, by itself, prove that a medicine caused the event; reports are reviewed alongside other clinical and safety information.

Bangladesh's national pharmacovigilance guideline states that healthcare professionals—including doctors, nurses and pharmacists—as well as community health workers, patients, consumers and members of the public can submit spontaneous reports. Patients should tell a healthcare professional at a health facility about a suspected reaction. If that is not immediately possible, the guideline allows a report to be made to a community health worker or directly to the DGDA's Adverse Drug Reaction Monitoring (ADRM) Cell. Healthcare professionals can use the reporting pathway or pharmacovigilance focal point at their facility, or contact the ADRM Cell through the channels described in the official guideline. Visit the DGDA website for official reporting information.

When reporting, provide as much accurate information as possible: the patient details requested by the form, suspected product, event description and timing, other medicines, relevant medical history and reporter contact details. The DGDA guideline describes confidentiality protections for report information. If you are unsure whether a symptom is medicine-related, report it as suspected rather than waiting until causation is proven.

9. How are reports used?

Safety teams look for patterns across reports and other evidence. They may assess whether the event is already known, whether similar cases have occurred, whether there are alternative explanations and whether further investigation is needed. A signal may lead to closer monitoring, updated product information, a safety communication or another regulatory action. One report is valuable, but it is not enough on its own to establish frequency or causality.

Key takeaways

  • A side effect is an unintended effect; an ADR is a harmful, unintended response with a suspected medicine-related cause.
  • An adverse event happens during treatment but does not automatically mean the medicine caused it.
  • Not every ADR is an allergy, and serious is not simply another word for severe.
  • Seek emergency help for breathing difficulty, tongue/throat swelling, collapse or a rapidly worsening blistering/peeling rash.
  • Record the details and report suspected reactions through an appropriate healthcare professional or Bangladesh's DGDA pharmacovigilance pathway.

Frequently asked questions

Are side effects and adverse drug reactions (ADRs) the same?

The terms overlap in everyday use, but they are not always identical. A side effect is an unintended effect associated with a medicine. An ADR is a harmful, unintended response at normal use for which a medicine-related cause is suspected.

What is the difference between an adverse event and an ADR?

An adverse event occurs during treatment or exposure and does not necessarily have a causal link to the medicine. An ADR is a harmful, unintended response for which a causal relationship is suspected.

Does every adverse drug reaction mean a medicine allergy?

No. Allergy involves the immune system. Many medicine-related reactions are not allergic and may be related to dose, pharmacological action, interactions or individual factors.

What is the difference between a serious and a severe reaction?

Severe usually describes symptom intensity. Serious is a formal classification based on outcomes such as death, life-threatening events, hospitalisation or significant disability. A healthcare professional should assess worrying symptoms.

What should I do if I think a medicine is causing a side effect?

Seek emergency care for serious warning signs. Otherwise, contact your doctor or pharmacist promptly, keep the medicine packaging and record the product, dose, timing and symptoms. Do not change or repeat a dose without advice.

Can patients report suspected adverse drug reactions in Bangladesh?

Yes. Bangladesh's national pharmacovigilance guideline allows patients, consumers and members of the public to submit spontaneous reports. Patients can contact a healthcare professional or use the reporting route described by the DGDA.
