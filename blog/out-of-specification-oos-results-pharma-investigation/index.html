Out-of-Specification (OOS) Results in Pharma: How Are Failed Tests Investigated?

What does an OOS result mean in pharmaceutical QC? Learn laboratory and manufacturing investigations, retesting rules, batch decisions and CAPA.

Englishবাংলা

A pharmaceutical quality-control test is designed to answer a precise question: does the material or product meet its approved specification? If a result falls outside that acceptance limit, it is an out-of-specification (OOS) result. It is a serious quality signal—but the number alone does not explain the cause.

For example, imagine a hypothetical tablet assay specification of 95.0–105.0% and a result of 93.0%. The result is outside the stated range. The team must preserve the original evidence and investigate whether the cause is analytical, sampling-related, material-related or linked to the manufacturing process. It must not simply repeat the test until a passing number appears.

If you have read the Quality Assurance vs Quality Control guide, this article takes a deeper look at one important part of that system: how an OOS result is investigated and how a batch decision is reached.

In short: An OOS result is a test result outside an approved acceptance criterion. The first result is generally treated as a suspected OOS result. After a thorough, documented investigation, it may be confirmed as OOS—or shown to have resulted from a clearly identified laboratory error.

1. What does OOS mean in pharmaceutical testing?

Specifications set the quality limits a material or product must meet. They may come from an approved product application, a drug master file, an official pharmacopoeia or a manufacturer's approved specification. OOS results can arise during testing of an active pharmaceutical ingredient (API), excipients, other components, in-process material, finished medicine or stability samples.

WHO distinguishes a suspected OOS result—the first result that has not yet been investigated and confirmed—from a confirmed OOS result, which remains outside the acceptance criteria after a thorough investigation. That distinction matters: a suspected result must never be ignored, but it should not be labelled a confirmed product failure before the evidence is assessed.

Examples include an assay result below its lower limit, dissolution that does not meet the approved acceptance criteria, an impurity above its limit, or a content-uniformity result outside its criteria. These are illustrative examples; the actual specification is product- and test-specific.

2. OOS, OOT and deviation: what is the difference?

OOT results deserve attention even when they pass. A gradual movement toward a limit may be an early warning that a method, material or process is changing. The exact definitions and alert limits should be set in the site's approved procedures.

3. Why can an OOS result happen?

An OOS result can come from more than one part of the testing and manufacturing system. Investigators should follow evidence rather than assume that the analyst or the batch is automatically at fault.

  • Laboratory or measurement factors: sample preparation or dilution errors, calculation mistakes, incorrect standards or reagents, instrument malfunction, failed system suitability, calibration problems, method performance or data-handling errors.
  • Sampling and material factors: a non-representative sample, mix-up, inadequate mixing, unsuitable sample storage, raw-material variability or a packaging/component issue.
  • Manufacturing or process factors: variation in blending, granulation, compression, coating, filling or another critical operation; equipment or maintenance problems; environmental conditions; or an uncontrolled process parameter.

One result may have several plausible explanations. A sound investigation tests those explanations against original data, batch records and scientific knowledge.

4. How is an OOS investigation carried out?

FDA's May 2022 OOS guidance describes a useful two-phase framework for chemistry-based pharmaceutical testing. WHO's 2024 good-practice guidance for pharmaceutical quality-control laboratories also sets out checks for suspected OOS results. The labels “Phase I” and “Phase II” are a way to explain the investigation; they should not be mistaken for a separate Bangladesh legal procedure.

Step 1: Protect the batch and preserve evidence

Report the result promptly through the site's approved procedure. The affected batch should remain on hold while its status is unresolved. Preserve original sample preparations when stable, raw data, chromatograms or spectra, worksheets, calculations, instrument records, audit trails, standards, reagents and relevant logs. Do not discard, overwrite or selectively transcribe inconvenient data.

Step 2: Phase I — laboratory assessment

The analyst and laboratory supervisor review the test before deciding whether additional testing is justified. They check whether the correct method and procedure were followed, whether calculations are correct, whether the instrument was qualified and calibrated, whether system suitability passed, and whether the correct standards, reagents, solvents, glassware and sample preparation were used. Raw data and electronic audit trails should be examined, not just the final reported number.

If a specific, scientifically supported laboratory error is identified, it must be documented and reviewed. Only then may the affected result be invalidated under the approved procedure, with any justified retest controlled and recorded. A vague suspicion that “the lab made a mistake” is not enough.

Step 3: Phase II — full investigation when no laboratory cause is established

If the initial assessment does not identify a clear laboratory cause, the quality unit coordinates a wider investigation. Relevant teams may include QC, QA, manufacturing, engineering, maintenance, warehouse, sampling and supplier quality. They review the batch record, process parameters, equipment history, deviations, raw-material and component data, environmental records, previous batches and any related products or manufacturing sites.

The investigation should identify an actual or probable root cause, assess whether other batches may be affected, and define corrective and preventive actions (CAPA). If the product has already been distributed, the impact assessment must also consider the distributed stock and any action required under the applicable local requirements.

5. Retesting and resampling: when are they appropriate?

These terms are not interchangeable:

  • Retesting means testing another portion of the original homogeneous sample. It may help investigate a suspected preparation or instrument issue when scientifically justified.
  • Resampling means testing additional units from the original sampling plan or collecting a new representative sample from the batch. It needs a documented reason and an appropriate sampling plan.

The number and purpose of any additional tests should be defined in advance by an approved procedure or investigation protocol. The plan must not be changed simply because results are inconvenient. If the original OOS result has no scientifically established cause, a later passing result does not automatically erase it. FDA describes repeated testing until a passing result is obtained while disregarding the OOS result as “testing into compliance”; this is not an acceptable substitute for investigation.

Remember: Retest and resample are investigation tools, not a way to manufacture a passing result. Preserve and report all valid results, including the original OOS result, unless a documented investigation establishes why a specific result is invalid.

6. What happens to the batch?

The quality unit reviews the complete evidence before authorising a batch disposition. A confirmed OOS result can mean the batch must be rejected. Reprocessing or rework, where permitted, must follow an approved, scientifically justified procedure and the material must meet the applicable specifications before any further use or release. A passing certificate alone cannot replace a complete batch-record review.

In the United States, 21 CFR 211.192 requires a thorough written investigation of unexplained discrepancies and specification failures, including related batches. Section 211.165(f) requires drug products that fail to meet established standards or specifications to be rejected. These are US requirements; they are cited here as a regulatory example, not as Bangladesh law.

7. What is the Bangladesh context?

Section 17 of Bangladesh's Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 2023 states that manufacturers and sellers must follow WHO Good Practices (GxP) for manufacturing, quality control, distribution, supply and storage of medicines. DGDA's National Drug Control Laboratory describes its quality policy as aligned with national and international standards, including ISO/IEC 17025, WHO and current GMP expectations.

In the official DGDA materials reviewed for this article, I did not identify a standalone public OOS investigation flowchart equivalent to the FDA guidance. Therefore, the FDA document and US 21 CFR provisions should not be presented as Bangladesh law. Manufacturers in Bangladesh should follow applicable DGDA directions, approved product specifications, WHO GxP expectations and their current approved SOPs. The exact legal and quality-system requirements depend on the product and activity.

8. Why root-cause analysis and CAPA matter

Closing one investigation is not enough if the same failure keeps returning. CAPA should address the demonstrated cause—for example, method robustness, equipment maintenance, training, sampling, raw-material control or a manufacturing parameter—and the organisation should check whether the action actually prevents recurrence. WHO recommends retaining all test data, documenting conclusions and reviewing the nature, number and root causes of OOS results as part of management review.

ICH Q9(R1) encourages science-based, risk-proportionate quality decisions. That means assessing the potential effect on patients and product quality, using the available evidence, documenting uncertainty and applying effort that matches the risk. Risk assessment must never be used to excuse a result or bypass an applicable requirement.

Key takeaways

  • An OOS result is a serious signal that requires a documented investigation.
  • Preserve the original sample, raw data and audit trail before additional testing.
  • Check laboratory causes first; expand to materials, sampling and manufacturing when needed.
  • Retesting and resampling need a scientific reason and an approved plan—never test into compliance.
  • Batch disposition and CAPA must be based on the complete evidence and applicable requirements.

Frequently asked questions

Does every OOS result mean the whole batch is defective?

No. The first result is usually treated as a suspected OOS result. A documented investigation must assess laboratory, sampling, material and manufacturing causes. A confirmed OOS result remains outside the approved criteria after thorough investigation.

Can a laboratory repeat the test until it passes?

No. Repeated testing until a passing result appears, while ignoring the original OOS result, is testing into compliance. Retesting must have a scientific purpose, a predefined procedure and documented quality-unit oversight.

What is the difference between retesting and resampling?

Retesting analyses another portion of the original homogeneous sample. Resampling collects additional units or a new representative sample from the batch. The reason and plan for either action must be justified and documented.

Who is responsible for an OOS investigation?

The analyst and laboratory supervisor begin the laboratory assessment. If no clear laboratory cause is found, the quality unit coordinates a wider investigation with relevant teams such as manufacturing, engineering, sampling and materials.

Can a batch be rejected before the investigation is finished?

A quality unit may place a batch on hold and may reject it when the evidence supports that decision. Rejection does not remove the need to investigate the cause or assess related batches and products.

Is OOS the same as out of trend (OOT)?

No. OOS means a result is outside an approved acceptance limit. OOT generally describes a result that may still be within specification but is unusual compared with its historical pattern.

Does Bangladesh have the same OOS rules as the United States?

No. FDA guidance and US 21 CFR provisions are US materials, not Bangladesh law. Section 17 of Bangladesh’s Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 2023 requires WHO Good Practices (GxP); manufacturers must also follow applicable DGDA requirements and approved procedures.
