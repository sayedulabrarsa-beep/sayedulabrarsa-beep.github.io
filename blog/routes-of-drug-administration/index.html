Routes of Drug Administration: Oral, Injection, Inhaled and More

Explore common routes of medicine administration and how each route affects delivery and absorption.

Englishবাংলা

By Sayedul Abrar, pharmacist · 5 December 2026

In simple terms: A route of administration is the path a medicine takes into or onto the body. The route affects where the medicine is delivered, how it is absorbed, how quickly it may act and what precautions are needed.

1. What is a route of administration?

A route of administration describes how a medicine is introduced into the body or applied to a body surface. The route is not the same as the dosage form: a tablet, solution, injection or patch describes the product form, while oral, intravenous or transdermal describes how it is used. The correct route is chosen according to the medicine's properties, the intended local or systemic effect, the patient's condition and practical safety considerations.

Routes are often grouped as enteral (through the gastrointestinal tract), parenteral (bypassing the gastrointestinal tract, commonly by injection), and routes such as inhalation, topical application and transdermal delivery. Classification conventions can differ between references, especially for routes that use the oral mucosa.

2. Enteral and oral-mucosal routes

Oral (by mouth)

Oral medicines are swallowed and pass through the gastrointestinal tract. They are convenient and generally suitable for people who can swallow safely, but absorption may vary with food, gut function, formulation and other medicines. Some orally absorbed drugs undergo first-pass metabolism in the intestinal wall and liver before reaching the systemic circulation.

Sublingual and buccal

Sublingual medicines are placed under the tongue; buccal medicines are placed between the cheek and gum. Suitable drugs can cross the oral mucosa and enter the circulation while partly avoiding the hepatic first-pass pathway. They must be used exactly as directed—do not chew or swallow a product intended to dissolve in place unless its instructions say otherwise.

Rectal and feeding-tube administration

Rectal products, such as suppositories, may be used for local effects or for systemic absorption when an appropriate product is available. Absorption can be variable, and not every medicine is suitable for this route. Some medicines are administered through a nasogastric or gastrostomy tube directly into the gastrointestinal tract; tube compatibility, formulation and flushing instructions must be checked by a healthcare professional.

3. Parenteral routes: injections and infusions

  • Intravenous (IV): delivered into a vein. It enters the systemic circulation directly, giving complete systemic bioavailability and allowing injection or infusion under appropriate clinical supervision. It also carries risks such as infection, phlebitis and administration errors.
  • Intramuscular (IM): injected into a muscle. The medicine is absorbed from muscle tissue; the rate depends on the formulation, blood flow and other factors. Some products are designed as depot injections for prolonged release.
  • Subcutaneous (SC): injected into the tissue beneath the skin. Absorption is generally slower than IV and can vary with local blood flow and the product. Insulin and some biologic medicines are commonly supplied for this route.
  • Intradermal (ID): placed within the skin, often for selected diagnostic tests or vaccines.

Other specialist routes include intrathecal (into cerebrospinal fluid), intra-articular (into a joint) and intraosseous (into bone marrow space). These are used only for specific clinical indications by trained professionals. Parenteral products must meet route-specific quality requirements, including sterility where applicable.

4. Inhaled, nasal, topical and transdermal routes

Inhalation

Inhaled medicines are breathed into the respiratory tract using devices such as metered-dose or dry-powder inhalers and nebulisers. Many are intended to act locally in the lungs, although some medicines can be absorbed systemically. Device technique matters: follow the product instructions and ask a pharmacist or clinician to check inhaler technique.

Intranasal

Nasal sprays or drops are administered into the nose. They may act locally, for example on nasal symptoms, or deliver selected medicines for systemic effects. Do not assume a nasal product is interchangeable with an inhaler or an oral product.

Topical versus transdermal

Topical medicines are applied to skin or another body surface mainly to produce an effect at that site, such as a cream for a skin condition or eye drops for an eye condition. Transdermal systems, commonly patches, are designed to pass medicine through the skin into the bloodstream for a systemic effect. A patch is not simply a topical cream in a different shape; its release and use instructions are product-specific.

5. Other local routes

Ophthalmic products are used in the eye, while otic products are used in the ear. Vaginal medicines may be used for local treatment and, in selected products, systemic delivery. These routes require products specifically formulated for the intended site. Never put ear drops in the eye or use a skin preparation in the eye unless the product is explicitly labelled for ophthalmic use.

6. How does the route affect medicine action?

The route can influence bioavailability, onset, duration, concentration at the target site and adverse-effect risk. IV administration places medicine directly into the circulation; oral administration requires release and absorption from the gastrointestinal tract; inhaled treatment can deliver medicine to the lungs; and topical treatment can concentrate exposure at a local surface. However, a route does not guarantee that one medicine will act faster or better than another—the drug, dose, formulation, device and patient all matter.

Route selection also depends on whether the patient can swallow, whether vomiting or unconsciousness is present, the required speed and duration of treatment, the need for local versus systemic action, and the risks and skills involved in administration.

7. Essential medicine-safety rules

  • Use a medicine only by the route stated on its label, package insert or prescription.
  • Never inject an oral liquid or tablet, swallow an inhalation product, or put a non-ophthalmic preparation into the eye.
  • Do not crush, split, open or mix a dosage form for tube administration unless product-specific guidance confirms it is safe.
  • Use the correct device and technique for inhalers, injections, eye drops and patches; ask a pharmacist or healthcare professional if unsure.
  • Do not change the route or dose yourself. Contact the prescriber or pharmacist if the prescribed route is not practical or cannot be used safely.

Remember

Route of administration is a core part of safe medicine use—not a convenience choice. The same active ingredient in different routes or formulations may not have the same dose, absorption or effect. Follow the product-specific instructions and seek professional advice before any change.

Frequently asked questions

What are the main routes of drug administration?

Common routes include oral, sublingual, buccal, rectal, intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous, inhaled, nasal, topical, transdermal, ophthalmic, otic and vaginal routes. Specialist routes are used for selected clinical purposes.

What is the difference between enteral and parenteral administration?

Enteral administration uses the gastrointestinal tract, such as swallowing a medicine or giving it through a feeding tube. Parenteral administration bypasses the gastrointestinal tract and commonly refers to injections such as IV, IM and SC.

Is intravenous administration always better or faster than oral administration?

No. IV administration delivers medicine directly into the circulation, but it is not automatically the best choice. Oral treatment is suitable for many conditions and may be safer and more convenient. The route depends on the medicine and clinical need.

What is the difference between topical and transdermal medicine?

Topical medicines are generally intended to act at the application site. Transdermal products, such as patches, are designed to deliver medicine through the skin into systemic circulation.

Are inhaled and nasal medicines the same?

No. Inhaled medicines are delivered into the respiratory tract, often toward the lungs. Intranasal medicines are administered into the nose and may act locally or systemically, depending on the product.

Can I use a medicine by another route if it is easier?

No. Use only the route stated in the prescription and product instructions. A different route can change absorption, dose, effectiveness and safety.
