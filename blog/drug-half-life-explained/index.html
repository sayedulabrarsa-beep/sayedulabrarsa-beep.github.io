Drug Half-Life: How Long Does a Medicine Stay in the Body?

Understand half-life, drug elimination and why dosing intervals differ between medicines.

Englishবাংলা

By Sayedul Abrar, pharmacist · 15 November 2026

In simple terms: Understand half-life, drug elimination and why dosing intervals differ between medicines.

1. What is half-life?

A medicine's elimination half-life is the time required for its amount or concentration in the body to fall by 50% during the elimination phase. If a concentration falls from 100 units to 50 units, one half-life has passed; it then falls to about 25 units after another half-life under simple first-order conditions.

2. Why does half-life matter?

Half-life helps clinicians understand how often a medicine may need to be taken, how long it may take to approach steady state with repeated doses, and how long it may remain after stopping. It is only one factor: the dose, formulation, therapeutic range, clinical response and patient factors also matter.

3. Does every medicine follow the same pattern?

No. Many medicines show first-order elimination over usual concentration ranges, meaning a constant fraction is removed per unit time. Some show capacity-limited or nonlinear elimination, where the fraction removed can change as concentration changes. A medicine may also have active metabolites or a long-lasting effect that does not match its plasma half-life.

4. What changes half-life?

Kidney and liver function, age, genetics, drug interactions and changes in body physiology can alter elimination. Extended-release formulations change how a drug enters the circulation, not necessarily its intrinsic elimination half-life.

5. A useful rule of thumb

For many medicines with first-order elimination, about four to five half-lives are needed for most of a dose to be eliminated. This is an approximation, not a safe rule for deciding when to stop treatment or take another dose.

6. Key takeaways

Half-life describes a 50% fall during elimination. It helps explain dosing and accumulation, but it does not by itself determine the correct dose or how long treatment should continue.

Frequently asked questions

Do all medicines behave in the same way?

No. Effects depend on the ingredient, formulation, route and the patient's circumstances.

Can I change a medicine dose myself?

No. Follow the prescription or label and consult a doctor or pharmacist before making changes.
