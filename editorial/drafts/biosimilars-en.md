# Biosimilars Explained: How Are They Compared with Original Biologics?

**The short answer:** A biosimilar is a biological medicine shown to be highly similar to an already authorised biological medicine called the reference product. It is not an ordinary generic copy. Because biological medicines are made using living cells and complex manufacturing processes, the goal is to demonstrate that any remaining differences do not create clinically meaningful differences in quality, safety or effectiveness.

Biologics and biosimilars are used in areas such as diabetes, cancer, inflammatory disease, anaemia and immune disorders. Understanding how they are evaluated helps pharmacists, clinicians and patients discuss treatment choices without confusing “similar” with “identical”.

*By Sayedul Abrar, Pharmacist · Editorial draft dated 24 November 2026*

## 1. What is a biological medicine?

A biological medicine, often called a biologic, is made using biological systems such as living cells or microorganisms. Many are proteins, including insulin, growth factors, erythropoietin and monoclonal antibodies. Their size, folded three-dimensional structure, sugar attachments and other molecular features can influence how they work.

A conventional small-molecule medicine is usually made through chemical synthesis and has a relatively simple, precisely defined structure. A protein medicine is much larger and more complex. Its final characteristics depend not only on the genetic sequence but also on the cell line, culture conditions, purification steps, formulation, storage and manufacturing controls.

Even the same reference biologic can show a degree of natural batch-to-batch variation. Manufacturers therefore control a defined range of quality attributes rather than expecting every molecule in every batch to be physically identical. This does not mean that quality can vary without limits: the process must consistently produce a medicine that meets approved specifications.

## 2. Biologic, biosimilar and generic: what is the difference?

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Reference biologic | The original biological medicine authorised on a full quality, safety and efficacy dossier. It is the comparator for a biosimilar. |
| Biosimilar | A biological medicine demonstrated to be highly similar to its reference product, with no clinically meaningful differences in safety and effectiveness. |
| Generic medicine | A small-molecule medicine that is generally required to have the same active substance as its reference and demonstrate the appropriate pharmaceutical and bioequivalence requirements. |

A biosimilar is not expected to be an exact molecular duplicate in the way that a conventional generic active ingredient is expected to match its reference. It must instead pass a rigorous, direct comparison with one selected reference product. The World Health Organization (WHO) specifically states that the generic-medicine approach is not suitable for licensing complex protein biosimilars.[1]

The word “similar” is not a shortcut for “close enough”. A product should not be called a biosimilar unless the required comparability exercise demonstrates the necessary similarity.

## 3. How is biosimilarity demonstrated?

The assessment is a planned, head-to-head comparability exercise. It is based on the totality of evidence: regulators consider the combined findings rather than relying on one laboratory test or one clinical study.

### Step 1 — Select and understand the reference product

The developer selects a suitable, authorised reference product and studies multiple batches to understand its quality profile and normal variability. The entire comparison must be anchored to the chosen reference product. If a reference product from another jurisdiction is used, the regulator decides whether additional bridging evidence is needed.

### Step 2 — Compare structure and quality

Sensitive analytical methods examine characteristics such as amino-acid sequence, molecular mass, higher-order structure, charge variants, glycosylation patterns, purity, aggregates, degradation products and other impurities. No single technique can describe every feature, so complementary or orthogonal methods are used.

The purpose is to identify differences, understand why they exist and decide whether they could affect biological activity, stability, safety or clinical performance. A difference is not automatically a problem, but an unexplained difference in a clinically relevant attribute needs investigation.

### Step 3 — Compare biological function

Functional assays test what the molecule does. Depending on the product, tests may examine receptor binding, enzyme activity, cell signalling, cell-based potency or antibody-dependent functions. These tests connect molecular structure with biological activity.

For example, two antibody products may have similar amino-acid sequences, but differences in a relevant structural feature could influence binding or an immune-cell function. Functional testing helps determine whether such a difference matters.

### Step 4 — Use nonclinical and clinical evidence appropriately

The WHO 2022 guideline describes a tailored, case-by-case approach. Pharmacokinetic (PK) studies assess how the body handles the product. Pharmacodynamic (PD) studies examine measurable biological effects when suitable markers exist. Immunogenicity is assessed when needed. Comparative clinical efficacy and safety studies may be required where meaningful uncertainty remains, but a large efficacy trial is not automatically necessary for every product if robust analytical, functional and other evidence already establishes similarity.[1,2]

This is not a lower safety standard. It is a more focused use of evidence: the question is whether the proposed biosimilar is sufficiently similar to the reference product, not whether the biosimilar must independently repeat every study that established the reference product’s original benefits.

## 4. Why does immunogenicity matter?

Immunogenicity is the ability of a biological medicine to trigger an immune response. A patient may develop anti-drug antibodies (ADAs). Some antibodies bind to the medicine without blocking its action; others may neutralise its biological activity. Depending on the product and the clinical situation, an immune response may alter drug exposure, reduce response or contribute to hypersensitivity or other adverse effects.

The risk is not determined by the word “biosimilar” alone. It can be influenced by the molecule, aggregates, impurities, expression system, formulation, excipients, container, route, dose, treatment duration and patient-related factors. The WHO guideline recommends a product-specific risk assessment and, when immunogenicity testing is needed, appropriately designed assays and clinical sampling.[1]

A 2025 meta-analysis by Ji and colleagues found that pharmacokinetic similarity studies can provide useful safety and immunogenicity information for monoclonal-antibody and fusion-protein biosimilars. The authors did not conclude that every product can be assessed in exactly the same way; the findings support a tailored evidence package.[6]

## 5. Are biosimilars interchangeable with their reference products?

The terms are often confused:

- **Switching** usually means a clinician-led decision to move a patient from a reference biologic to a biosimilar, or vice versa.
- **Substitution** usually means a pharmacy supplies one product instead of another without first consulting the prescriber.
- **Interchangeability** has a specific regulatory meaning that differs by jurisdiction.

In the United States, the FDA has a separate legal designation for an “interchangeable biosimilar”. Pharmacy-level substitution also depends on state law. FDA-approved biosimilars that do not carry this additional designation are still considered as safe and effective as their reference products.[3]

In the European Union, the European Medicines Agency and Heads of Medicines Agencies stated in 2022 that EU-approved biosimilars are interchangeable with their reference medicines or an equivalent biosimilar. Decisions about pharmacy-level substitution remain the responsibility of individual Member States.[4]

These examples must not be transferred automatically to another country. WHO explains that national regulatory authorities determine local interchangeability and substitution arrangements.[1] A patient should not change a prescribed biologic or assume that a pharmacy may substitute it without checking the applicable local rules and the prescriber’s instructions.

A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis of 31 studies involving 21 biosimilars and 5,252 switched patients found no meaningful difference in the pooled safety outcomes examined between switched and non-switched groups. The review supports confidence in studied switching situations, but it does not prove that every product, patient or switching pattern is identical, nor does it establish local substitution law.[5]

## 6. Why does monitoring continue after approval?

Approval is not the end of safety evaluation. Rare events, long-term effects, manufacturing changes and product-specific problems may become clearer during routine use. WHO recommends post-marketing pharmacovigilance, risk management and product traceability for biosimilars.[1]

When reporting a suspected problem, useful details include the product’s brand name, international nonproprietary name (INN), manufacturer, batch or lot number, dose, route, date of use and the clinical event. These details help investigators distinguish products and trace a potential quality or safety issue. A report raises a concern; it does not by itself prove that the medicine caused the event.

## 7. What should healthcare professionals and patients remember?

1. Confirm the exact product, brand, strength, dosage form and route prescribed.
2. Follow the product-specific storage, preparation, administration and device instructions. Do not assume that two products use identical devices or handling steps.
3. Record the brand and batch or lot number when a biologic is administered, where the clinical system allows it.
4. Discuss a proposed switch with the treating clinician and explain the reason for the change.
5. Report suspected adverse reactions or product-quality concerns through the appropriate national system. Do not wait for proof of causation.
6. Do not stop, start or substitute a biologic based only on an online article.

### Bangladesh regulatory context: an important source-status note

WHO Bangladesh reported that DGDA launched a **Guideline for Registration of Biosimilar Products in 2018**.[7] DGDA also lists a document titled **Guidelines for Registration of Biosimilar Products in Bangladesh 2025** in its public marketing-authorisation guideline collection. However, DGDA’s public news listing includes a notice dated **17 August 2025** inviting comments on a *draft* “Biosimilar Product Registration Guideline-2025”. The public pages reviewed for this draft do not clearly state the final adoption date, effective date or whether the 2025 document formally supersedes the 2018 guideline.

DGDA also lists DGDA-RIMS as the online registration system for vaccine and biosimilar products.[8] Because the final status of the 2025 guideline could not be established conclusively from the public material reviewed, this article does not state a current Bangladesh-specific approval pathway, interchangeability rule or pharmacy-substitution permission. Applicants and healthcare professionals should confirm the applicable requirements directly with DGDA before relying on a particular version.

## Frequently asked questions

### 1. Is a biosimilar the same as a generic medicine?

No. A generic is generally a small-molecule medicine with the same active substance as its reference. A biosimilar is a complex biological medicine shown to be highly similar to a reference biologic through a dedicated comparability exercise.

### 2. Does “not identical” mean a biosimilar is less safe?

No. Approved biosimilars must meet the regulator’s standard for high similarity and no clinically meaningful differences. Minor molecular differences may exist, but they must be evaluated for their relevance to quality, safety and effectiveness.

### 3. Do all biosimilars need a large clinical efficacy trial?

Not necessarily. The amount and type of clinical evidence depend on the molecule, analytical and functional results, remaining uncertainties and the regulator’s requirements. PK/PD and immunogenicity data may be important; comparative efficacy studies are tailored to the evidence.

### 4. Can a biosimilar cause an immune response?

Any biological medicine can trigger an immune response. The relevant question is whether the biosimilar shows an unacceptable difference in immunogenicity compared with its reference product and whether any immune response affects safety or effectiveness.

### 5. Can a patient switch from a reference biologic to a biosimilar?

Switching has been studied for several products and the evidence is generally reassuring. The decision should still consider the individual product, indication, patient, prescriber’s plan, traceability and local rules.

### 6. Are biosimilars automatically interchangeable in Bangladesh?

This draft does not make that claim. DGDA’s public materials show a 2018 guideline, a 2025 guideline listing and a 2025 draft-consultation notice, but the final status and local substitution arrangements need confirmation from DGDA.

### 7. Why is the batch number important?

Biological products are complex and must be traceable. Recording the brand, manufacturer and batch or lot number helps connect a suspected adverse event or quality concern to the specific product supplied.
